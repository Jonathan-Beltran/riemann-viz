A visual explainer

The Riemann Hypothesis

Mathematicians have been stuck on this question since 1859. Solving it earns a $1,000,000 prize. At its heart is a simple question: is there a pattern to the prime numbers? You don't need any math beyond arithmetic to follow along.

1

Primes are the atoms of numbers

A prime is a whole number bigger than 1 that you can only divide evenly by 1 and itself: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13… Every other number is built by multiplying primes together, the way molecules are built from atoms (12 = 2 × 2 × 3). Hover over any square to see what it's made of.

Prime Composite (made from smaller primes)

Look at the highlighted squares. Sometimes primes come in clusters, and sometimes there are long gaps. They look almost random. Drag the slider up and two things show up. The primes get rarer: about 1 in 4 numbers up to 100 is prime, about 1 in 7 up to 5,000, and fewer than 1 in 10 up to 100,000. And they line up in vertical stripes, because apart from 2 and 5, every prime ends in 1, 3, 7 or 9. Is there any deeper rule behind them?

2

Counting primes: a staircase

We can't predict exactly where the next prime will be. But we can ask how many there are below some number x. Mathematicians call this count π(x) (π here is just a name, it has nothing to do with circles). Every time we pass a prime, the count goes up by one. So the graph looks like a staircase.

As a teenager, Carl Friedrich Gauss guessed a smooth curve that follows this staircase. Drag the slider to zoom out and see how well the guesses do.

Actual prime count π(x) Simple guess x ÷ ln(x) Gauss's better guess Li(x)
Show as a table

Zoom out to 10 million. The aqua curve (Li) sits almost exactly on the staircase. Primes look random up close, but from far away they follow a very smooth law. This is the Prime Number Theorem, and it was proved in 1896.

3

The real question: how big is the error?

Gauss's curve is close, but it isn't perfect. The gap between the true count and the guess goes up and down unevenly. The Riemann Hypothesis is, in plain words, a promise about this gap:

The error never gets bigger than roughly √x. Primes are as evenly spread as they could possibly be. They are random, but in a very well-behaved way.
Error: actual count − Li(x) The safe zone the Riemann Hypothesis promises

Up to 10 million, the error stays far inside the band. Computers have checked much further than this. But checking examples is not a proof. Nobody has shown that the error stays inside the band forever. The safe zone shown is √x·ln(x)/8π, which Lowell Schoenfeld proved in 1976 is equivalent to the hypothesis for x ≥ 2657.

4

Riemann's map: the zeta function

In 1859 Bernhard Riemann found an unexpected way into the problem. He studied the function

ζ(s) = 1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + 1/4s + …

Leonhard Euler had already shown that this sum is secretly made of primes: ζ(s) = 1/(1−2−s) × 1/(1−3−s) × 1/(1−5−s) × … Riemann fed it complex numbers, which are points on a 2D map instead of a number line. Then he asked: where on the map does ζ equal zero?

Zeros of ζ ("the interesting ones") "Trivial" zeros at −2, −4, −6… Critical strip

Hover either panel to follow a height on the map.

The Riemann Hypothesis: every interesting zero of ζ lies exactly on the line where the real part is ½.

Every zero found so far sits exactly on that line, and over 10 trillion of them have been checked. Nobody has found one that is off the line, and nobody has proved one can't be.

5

Why the zeros matter: the music of the primes

Here is the surprising part. Each zero acts like a musical note, a wave with its own pitch. Riemann showed that if you start with a smooth curve and add up these waves, you rebuild the prime staircase exactly. The zeros are the primes, written in a different form.

(To keep the formula clean, this staircase steps up by ln(p) at each prime p, and a little at prime powers like 4, 8, 9. The jumps happen in the same places as before.)

Press play. With zero waves you only get a smooth ramp. As waves are added, the corners sharpen and the curve snaps onto the steps, at exactly the primes.

6

Why the line at ½ matters

How tall a wave gets depends on its zero's position left to right. A zero at real part ½ makes a wave about √x tall, so it gets smaller and smaller compared with x. That is the "safe zone" from step 3. If even one zero sat farther right, its wave would grow faster, and the primes would be much messier than we believe.

So, in one sentence

The Riemann Hypothesis says the zeros of the zeta function all sit on one straight line. If that is true, the primes, which look random, are spread as evenly as possible, and the error in our prime-counting formulas stays small forever. Thousands of published results begin with "assuming the Riemann Hypothesis…". Nobody has proved it yet.
